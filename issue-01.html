Feature 01 Grid · Power

Why the substation became a landmark

For a century the grid hid in the hedges. Three engineers decided it should be the view instead.

PHOTO
Hugo Sale
September 21, 2026 · 11 min read

There is a moment, walking south from the river at dusk, when the substation stops looking like a warning and starts looking like a building. Its transformers hum behind a lattice of steel; a run of ceramic insulators catches the last of the light like a shelf of trophies. Nobody designed it to be beautiful. That, the engineers here will tell you, was the mistake worth fixing.

Infrastructure has spent most of a century apologising for itself. We buried the cables, fenced the plant, and planted fast-growing conifers in front of anything that buzzed. The result is a built world we rely on completely and refuse to look at — a landscape of shrugging grey boxes designed, above all, to be ignored.

The case for showing your working

The counter-movement is small but loud. In three sites across the south-east, engineers and architects have started treating the grid as something to be seen — painting gantries, uplighting cooling towers, cutting viewing slots into perimeter walls. The bill is trivial next to the build. The change in how people treat the thing is not.

"A fence says keep out. A good facade says look — and people did."

— Site engineer, Substation "A"

What surprised the operators was not the visitors — though there are now visitors — but the vandalism that stopped. A structure people find handsome is a structure people leave alone. Graffiti fell. Fly-tipping at the perimeter fell. The maintenance crews, oddly, reported taking more care too.

Three that got it right

Below, the shortlist from this issue's field trips — the full notes, drawings and a map are in the members' edition.

  1. 01 Battersea "A" — floodlighting turned a maintenance liability into the neighbourhood's favourite skyline.
  2. 02 Greenwich reach — a perimeter wall with viewing slots, so the plant reads as a colonnade.
  3. 03 Kent interconnector — gantries painted a single confident orange, visible for miles.

None of it is complicated. That is rather the point. The technology of caring what infrastructure looks like is a century old; what was missing was permission. This issue is an argument that the permission is overdue.
